IranSignal analysis: sequencing matters

Political contact is necessary but does not create an enforceable commitment by itself. If each side requires the other to complete its obligations first, even a detailed proposal can leave the dispute unresolved. The useful questions concern the order of actions, who verifies compliance, and what happens if implementation stops. The evidence reviewed for this edition does not establish a final joint text answering those questions.

Three competing paths

A limited, phased arrangement could let each side test compliance through small, verifiable steps. Alternatively, talks could continue while the unresolved sequence of concessions sustains attrition. A third path is renewed escalation, where failed contacts or an incident narrow the space for compromise. These are analytical alternatives, not confirmed developments or timed forecasts.

Evidence that would change the assessment

An agreed text, implementation schedule confirmed by the principal parties, and independent verification of the first action would strengthen the limited-arrangement interpretation. Repeated meetings without a testable outcome would fit the attrition interpretation more closely. Assessing escalation requires independently corroborated operational evidence. Harsh language or an unverified nuclear inventory alone does not establish a strike decision or weapon production.

Assessment

The question today is how a possible negotiation becomes an implementable agreement. Continued diplomacy can coexist with an unresolved central dispute. Until commitments, sequencing and verification appear together, continued bargaining is the more defensible working description. Observable implementation would justify revising that assessment.
